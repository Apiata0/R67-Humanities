We are learning to: choose and use language features and line breaks to help a reader see, hear and feel our poem’s meaning.
I DO · Notice the difference

A poem can tell a story, but it also uses sound, images, repetition, line breaks or figurative language.

Story-like:
I went to my nana’s house.
We ate dinner and talked.
I felt happy when I left.
Poetic choices:
At Nana’s table, stories steamed
beside the taro, warm as hands.
I carried her laughter home.

Point out the image, comparison, sound and line breaks. The poem still shares an event, but the language helps us feel it.

WE DO · Upgrade one line together

Start with: “I missed my home.”

Ask: What could the speaker see, hear or carry? What image could show missing home?

Possible class revision: “Home hummed softly in the shell I kept.”

Students name a choice: sound pattern, image, personification or a more precise word.

If I Were… poem

Imagine you are a person, animal or object. What would you see, hear, feel or do?

  1. Choose what you will become: If I were a pōhutukawa tree… or If I were a school gate…
  2. Make a quick list of what you might see, hear, feel and do.
  3. Write at least 3 short stanzas. Start each stanza with “If I were…” and add a new idea.
  4. Reread it. Does each idea fit the person, animal or object you chose?
If I were a __________,
I would __________.
I would hear __________,
and I would __________.
Model · three short stanzas

If I were the classroom door,
I would swing open wide.

I would hear busy footsteps,
and hold one last goodbye.

I would stay after the bell,
until everyone found their way.

Make your own ideas. You can choose a different topic.

Stretch: Keep the same “If I were…” opening, but make each stanza reveal a different feeling or point of view.

Colour poem

Choose one colour. Collect four things that are that colour. Add a describing word and an action or detail to each line.

  1. Write your colour as the title.
  2. List four things you connect with that colour.
  3. Choose a precise adjective for each thing.
  4. Write one clear image in each line. Reread and check that the colour connects all four lines.
[Adjective] __________ [action or detail].
[Adjective] __________ [action or detail].
[Adjective] __________ [action or detail].
[Adjective] __________ [action or detail].
Model · Blue

Deep ocean curls around the shore.
Cool shade rests under the pōhutukawa.
Clear sky opens above the field.
Faded denim warms in the afternoon sun.

Stretch: Make the four images build a mood or tell a tiny story, instead of being a list.

Found poem

A found poem is made from words in another text. Use only words from the short passage below. You may move them into a new order and choose your own line breaks.

Source passage · The spare chair

After the rain, the courtyard shone. A red jacket waited on a bench. Someone had left half an apple beside it. The bell rang, shoes hurried, and voices filled the doorway. Near the wall, one empty chair stayed in a patch of sunlight. A quiet hand moved the chair closer.

  1. Read the passage twice. Circle or copy words that make you picture a moment or feeling.
  2. Choose at least 8 words or short phrases from the passage.
  3. Arrange them into 4 or more poem lines. You may leave out words and change the order, but do not add new words.
  4. Give the poem a title. Check every word against the source passage.
Model · words found in the passage

After the rain,
a red jacket waited
on a bench.
One empty chair
stayed in the sunlight.

Stretch: Choose words that hint at a story without explaining it. Let your reader infer why the chair was moved.

Calligram · make the words form a shape

A calligram is a poem whose layout matches its meaning. Choose an object, place or memory that matters to you, then arrange your words to form its shape.

  1. Choose one subject, such as a shell, doorway, tree, wave or keepsake.
  2. List 6–10 precise words or short phrases that help a reader imagine it.
  3. Arrange the words so their outline or pattern looks like your subject. Make the words do the drawing.
  4. Read the poem. Add or change words if the shape is clear but the meaning is not.
Model · a leaf shape
              bright leaf
          listening in the wind
       holding rain, sheltering birds
          home above our path
              old leaf

This is one possible layout. Make a shape that fits your own subject.

You can build the shape with spaces and line breaks in a Google Doc, or write it on paper, take a clear photo and insert the photo into your Doc.

Stretch: Use the size, direction or repetition of the words to show movement or a change in meaning.

Haiku · make a clear image in three lines

Write about one small moment in nature or everyday life. A traditional English classroom haiku often uses 5 syllables, then 7, then 5.

  1. Choose one clear moment you can picture.
  2. Write three short lines: 5 syllables, 7 syllables, 5 syllables.
  3. Clap or say each line slowly to check the syllables.
  4. Swap a word if the count does not fit. Keep the image clear; do not add extra words just to fill the count.
Line 1 · 5 syllables: ____________________
Line 2 · 7 syllables: ____________________
Line 3 · 5 syllables: ____________________
Model · Rain at home

Rain taps on the glass (Rain 1 · taps 2 · on 3 · the 4 · glass 5)
Small silver drops slide slowly (Small 1 · sil 2 · ver 3 · drops 4 · slide 5 · slow 6 · ly 7)
Clouds cover the hill (Clouds 1 · cov 2 · er 3 · the 4 · hill 5)

Stretch: Show a change between the first and last line. Use a surprising detail instead of explaining the feeling.
